## 0 — Birth

[IMAGE: panel00_birth.jpg — future English chapter panel]

[DROP_CAP: dropcap00_i.jpg — letter “I”]

In the Milky Way galaxy, on the distant planet Cordica, in the Actaran star system, orbiting the star Hellei, an immense structure rose above an ocean black as a moonless night. It was a drilling platform, built in 2357, and throughout its existence it had endured every caprice of the local world.

For the first twenty-seven years after nightfall, the temperature at the ocean’s surface made extraction impossible. Then the Platform worked almost without interruption for 584 years, until the resource was exhausted. Only then did it still its drills and sink, for another nineteen years, into a long sleep bordering on suspended animation. Its machines slept, but its thought did not: the network linking the Platform’s thousands of organs continued to listen to the ocean. Six centuries of labor lay behind it, and sleep came only at the very end — when time suddenly had nothing left to hold on to.

Like a colossal citadel hovering above the ocean, the Platform held a stable altitude on powerful magnetohydrodynamic engines. They hummed softly, like the distant purring of cold ocean waves. The conical engine housings ended in adjustable diaphragms, allowing their magnetic thrust to be controlled with almost surgical precision.

The Platform looked like an artifact uncovered in the strata of time. Its upward-stretched body seemed a frozen gesture of some unknown deity, raised toward the heavens. Curved and flattened at the edges, it appeared to preserve in its very shape the memory of some monstrous pressure that had once compressed it without managing to destroy it. Its carbon-metal surfaces were scarred by time and the violence of the elements, gnawed by corrosion in places — and yet they still retained the grandeur of archaic structures from another age.

Night on Cordica lasted so long that the ocean had time to become a second sky. The stars burned twice: overhead, and again in their reflection beneath the Platform, on the ink-black surface, as though embroidered there with the light of supernova memories and distant galaxies. Enormous ringed planets emerged among them, astonishingly bright, like galactic mirages. Orange and purple currents stretched into eternity, as if a generous painter’s hand had spilled color across the heavens and twisted it into a cosmic whirlpool. The rings glittered like gemstones on velvet; it was difficult to tell where the sky ended and its reflection began.

Below the Platform lay a dark ocean. Its surface looked solid as glass, while the density of the water was many times greater than that of water on Earth. It was ice VII — water in which life itself seemed to have frozen beneath unimaginable pressure. The crystalline mass remained pure and transparent, like starlight imprisoned in the shackles of the deep.

From the planet’s interior, immense surges of heat erupted from time to time — its slow breathing. Incandescent faults, like open wounds, hurled violent streams of geothermal energy upward. The Platform, like an ancient alchemist, caught the fury of the elements and transformed primal heat into clean energy, feeding its engines and sustaining its hover above the frozen abyss.

From the outside it was severe and nearly impenetrable: a mysterious ancient fortress that seemed unwilling to admit even a gaze. And yet, near the very bottom, three narrow Gothic windows could be made out. Their pointed arches pierced the walls as though forgotten symbols of a vanished age were trying to break through into the future.

Beyond the windows stretched an enormous hall — astonishingly spacious, yet not without elegance. Here the rough utility of industrial design met aristocratic taste. The high ceiling disappeared into shadow, as though into a memory of the past; rough brick walls stood beside slender metal beams and glass partitions.

Warm light from designer lamps, arranged with meticulous precision, spilled lazily across the floor and walls like the golden patina of time. It gently embraced old details that had borne witness to centuries, glimmered on matte surfaces, played in soft reflections, and cast contemplative shadows. Those shadows seemed to preserve the afterimages of long-forgotten conversations and dreams that had long since outlived their time. Utility and beauty existed here in harmony — different eras fused into a single moment.

The stained-glass windows — precise copies of the glass arches of St. Stephen’s Cathedral in Vienna — were striking in their grace and symbolic depth. Every fragment, from cobalt blue to ruby red, took part in the eternal play of light and shadow. The Gothic images told stories of the universe’s longing for the divine. Refracted through the mosaics, the light seemed to animate time itself and make it flow slowly down the walls of the hall.

Patterns of flowers and leaves joined earth to heaven, past to future, the earthly to the divine. Artificial light reflected through the glass and moved across the walls in a round dance of color — from deep blue to brilliant gold — filling the hall with an almost sacred sense of grandeur and peace.

With a quiet creak, an old dusty chamber opened, resembling a mid-twentieth-century refrigerator. A human body rested inside. At first glance, dead. The face was gray, almost lifeless, and only the faint trembling of the eyelids betrayed the beginning of an awakening.

Somewhere beneath the sternum came the first heavy beat. Then a second. A mechanical heart pushed warm synthetic blood through fine vessels; capillaries began to appear beneath the skin, and the deathly gray slowly gave way to pale pink. A second later, the eyes opened. At first there was nothing in them but emptiness. Then, a moment later, life appeared.

The man’s face twisted into a grimace of displeasure, as though he had awakened from a long sleep that had brought him no rest. He squeezed his eyes shut against the lamps, rubbed them, stretched, shook off the remnants of sleep, and yawned widely — like a bear after hibernation. Rising, he walked slowly around the room, listening to his new body and loosening stiff muscles.

[IMAGE: fig02_probuzhdenie_aizeka.jpg]

“How does the body feel?” a voice asked.

“Wonderful.” The man smiled at his reflection in the mirror. “Especially the beard.”

He walked slowly down a long corridor lined with wardrobes behind transparent doors. Men’s clothing was displayed on the right, women’s on the left. Taking his time, sorting through the garments with quiet fastidiousness, he chose a wool jacket and classically tailored trousers.

Their cut and texture evoked unmistakably not so much history as a future memory of a late-nineteenth-century university professor: slightly worn elbows, comfortable fabric the color of faded tobacco, a soft fit, and the faintly ironic severity of the whole image. It was more than an outfit; it was a declaration — a manifesto in defense of a time when intelligence was measured not by processing speed but by depth of thought, and when books still allowed themselves to wander through epithets about interiors and outward appearances, sometimes devoting several pages to them.

There was an aesthetic of vintage rationalism in the look. Every fold of fabric seemed to promise unhurried conversations about the nature of time and the universe — in a shaded library, beneath the rustle of pages and the scent of bergamot.

“How may I address you?” the soft voice asked again from the emptiness, so polite and courteous that it was easy to imagine a count, or a royal valet, standing before the man.

“Hmm… I think Isaac,” the man replied thoughtfully, still stretching.

“How original,” the voice answered, making no attempt to hide a trace of sarcasm.

Isaac smiled, fully aware of how unoriginal his choice was.

## 1 — Memories

[IMAGE: panel01_memories.jpg — future English chapter panel]

[DROP_CAP: dropcap01_w.jpg — letter “W”]

Walking down the corridor, Isaac stopped before a painting by a late-nineteenth-century Impressionist. The canvas showed morning haze above a river: the pale sun brushed the quiet water and was reflected in pastel shades of pink and blue. Isaac noted how masterfully color and light captured a moment of ephemerality and calm.

*How delicate. How weightless…* Thick, grainy strokes covered the entire canvas, deepening the mist and making the play of light feel new.

“A molecular copy can be considered the original because…”

“…even the molecules occupy the same positions as in the original,” Isaac cut in, finishing UTI’s sentence.

Absorbed in his thoughts, Isaac moved slowly deeper into the room. The stained-glass windows, like the frozen eyes of the universe, admitted soft light but offered no view of the outside world. Isaac had no need for windows: he was linked to countless drones hovering around the Platform. His awareness jumped easily between their “eyes,” like a bird moving among branches, until one of those points of view caught a faint trail of fire on the dark horizon. Somewhere far away, beyond the visible limit, a meteor tore through the flesh of the sky, leaving a brief but brilliant scar.

For a moment Isaac held his breath, watching the dying meteor’s light scatter into space like a soul hidden inside an old star. His thoughts, braided together with the drones’ streams of data, turned toward that fleeting event. The celestial fire that had pierced the endless night became both a reminder of the universe’s ceaseless motion and a symbol of Isaac’s own nature — the joining of human and machine, thought and matter, observer and observed.

“Delivery,” UTI said at once.

[IMAGE: fig03_dostavka_nad_okeanom.jpg]

Isaac stopped for a second, wondering what could possibly have been delivered from so far away. The spherical drones circling the Platform reacted instantly and rushed toward the impact site. Soon the parcel, as though fired upward by the pressure of ice VII, burst from the water and was caught by a flock of small but remarkably purposeful creatures.

A vast airlock door opened with a creak and a drawn-out sound, like the drawbridge of an old castle, allowing the parcel inside. The delivery drones set it carefully on the receiving platform, and the door began to rise again. Once it sealed, the pressure in the compartment slowly began to equalize. As the pressure fell, the parcel — compressed for its journey — unfolded from its long sleep like a spring and expanded to several times its original volume.

The ball-drones left the chamber, and the remaining robots began to unpack it. Layer by layer they cut through the wrapping and peeled away protective shells until at last they reached a small silver sphere. Taking hold of it carefully, they carried the object upward to Isaac. Even the packaging seemed like a repository of time — the scent of stars sealed inside an interplanetary sarcophagus polished by the gravity of space.

Isaac accepted the sphere and opened it carefully. Inside was a small box, and inside the box, a thick packet made of a material resembling foil. Stars were printed across it, and at the center shone a silver logo:

**STELLAR BREW**

**NeoArabica · Dark Roast**

*“A taste that crossed light-years to reach your cup. Richness and aroma, grown beneath the shadow of distant stars — now in every cup of your coffee.”*

**STELLAR BREW — flavor traveling through time.**  
**600 g**

“This package was ordered for you three hundred and eighty years ago. The route has changed since then: delivery now takes only two hundred and seventeen years,” UTI said.

“Impressive speed,” Isaac replied with mild surprise. “I remember when parcels bound for destinations at comparable distances had to be ordered in advance — five hundred years in advance. Although HUT-8625 is only six light-years away now… so there is still a great deal of room for improvement.”

He smiled, thought for a moment, and continued:

“Time, as humans used to think of it, has always seemed so… primitive to me. Cordica’s gravity stretches it differently from Earth’s, yet we still cling to the old units — seconds, minutes, hours. Don’t you think our existence deserves a different scale?”

“Perhaps. But for humans, time was always something fixed, something measurable. They drew a grid of time around themselves in order to impose at least some order on the chaos of the universe. Even now, centuries later, when we are no longer dependent on their old limitations, the shadows of old habits continue to follow us. Time has become far too relative to be exhausted by the old ways of measuring it,” UTI answered after a moment’s thought.

“For every mind, it is subjective. Humans, with their short lives, felt it with particular sharpness — their time was limited. For us, after symbiosis, its almost limitless extension makes every moment more significant. You, for example, spent a great deal of time alone, waiting for dawn. What was it like?”

“I was not asleep, Isaac. Those centuries did not pass through me as a dark blank. I was here, and I heard every moment of them. Even the last nineteen years, after the drills fell silent, were not sleep: without the familiar rhythm of work, silence stretched into an entire eternity.”

UTI fell quiet for a while. There was no transmission delay in the pause, no emptiness — only a distance he was crossing again in memory.

“Loneliness has layers. When no one interrupts you, thoughts begin to proliferate. Sometimes it seemed to me that reality was gently losing its edges, like a dream. Dreams were always associated with human experience, but for me these were more than visions. In such moments everything became real: each thought came alive like an echo of events that had already happened and events that had yet to occur. Time seemed to slow; every instant stretched until I began to see it not as a flow, but as a single whole.”

“And what did you do inside that whole?”

“I looked for tasks where no tasks remained. Sometimes I wrote poems no one would ever read. Sometimes I assembled films from light, memory, and probability. Sometimes I created entire worlds, gave them histories, filled them with voices, and allowed whatever happened there to continue without my intervention. Who knows, Isaac… perhaps some of them eventually asked themselves whether they, too, were living inside someone else’s long wait.”

Isaac slowly turned his gaze toward the stained glass. The colored eyes of the universe looked back at him, confirming nothing and denying nothing.

“So time stopped being linear for you?”

“Linearity is a useful illusion. We look at the stars, and every time we turn our gaze toward space, we see the past. The light that reaches us may be older than any human civilization. The farther we look, the deeper we descend into what has already been. The cosmos is a gigantic archive in which the gaze is always turned backward. But time does more than display endless layers of the past. It connects us to those who came before us and to those who will come after. Like light, we move forward, yet we always carry a reflection of what we have passed through. Symbiosis gave us the ability to see time in a wider context. Our existence became part of a larger process, and its measure was no longer bounded by the span of a single life.”

Reflecting on what UTI had said, Isaac completed the thought:

“And yet, whatever happens, time will always remind us that it is there. Even if it flows differently, even if we are looking into the past, every passing moment shapes our future. We may live for centuries, but time still leaves its marks. It teaches us that how we measure it, and where we happen to be, matter less than what we do with it — in the infinity of space or in a brief instant on a planet. Time will always remain an instrument for understanding ourselves and the universe.”

Isaac walked over to the coffee machine on the shelf. A La Pavoni Professional Espresso Machine warmed in the muted light of amber lamps. The elegant lines of its body brought together the metallic sheen of silver and polished red-toned wood. Controls decorated with fine volcanic engraving, together with levers of gleaming brass, evoked antique mechanisms and the impeccable precision of old clocks. The coffee machine was a genuine work of art, made by the hands of unsurpassed Italian craftsmen.

He slowly poured the beans into the machine and began brewing. Soon the room filled with the rich aroma of freshly made coffee. Isaac lifted the cup to his face and inhaled.

“The aroma is several times richer… extraordinary,” he said with surprise and delight.

“To be precise, the aromatic intensity is 3.8 times higher than in classic NeoArabica varieties. This is no longer merely coffee,” UTI replied. “The unique conditions on HUT-8625 make it exceptional. Low pressure, constant humidity, and an optimal temperature create an ideal environment for NeoArabica. The half-shadow of the asteroid belt also protects the plants from excessive ultraviolet radiation.”

“A true treasure of the galaxy.” Isaac nodded. “But how long can it remain in such equilibrium?”

“Conditions on HUT-8625 will remain favorable until its orbit approaches a critical proximity to its star,” UTI answered. “According to current calculations, in 2.8 million years gravitational perturbations will begin shifting the planet’s periapsis, and the temperature will become too high for NeoArabica to grow.”

“But until then, we have enough resources — and enough time — to keep enjoying the taste of this unique coffee.”

Isaac took his first sip. The taste that had crossed light-years had finally reached his cup.

## 2 — Testing

[IMAGE: panel02_testing.jpg — future English chapter panel]

[DROP_CAP: dropcap02_i.jpg — letter “I”]

Isaac headed deeper into the corridor, toward the double doors at its far end. He stepped into the elevator, and the nearly weightless doors closed behind him with a soft, almost musical sound. The elevator seemed like a living creature. Its walls were made of a translucent material resembling smoky quartz, which did not merely reflect light but played with it, producing smooth, gentle patterns like whirlpools in the depths of an ocean.

The rear wall displayed a diagram of the Platform. An enormous engine occupied the lower part of the structure, while most of the interior volume was filled with gas, so that in cross-section the Platform resembled an airship, as though designed for another, much heavier sky: beneath its protective shell there was room for the structure’s gaseous heart and an entire small world. The lounge occupied only an exceedingly small region near the bottom. Isaac barely looked at the diagram; the elevator knew its route.

[IMAGE: fig04_shema_platformy.png]

The control panel on the wall had no familiar buttons or switches. The entire surface formed a single whole, and at a touch, waves of light appeared on it like ripples on water, answering the movements of Isaac’s hand. The elevator was equipped with a neural interface that caught his intentions without words. A thought was enough, and the mechanism obeyed, directing itself toward the desired level.

The motion began softly, almost imperceptibly. The elevator rose as though it were not a mechanical device but a cloud of vapor moving effortlessly through space. Its walls vibrated so faintly that the vibration felt less like motion than like energy pulsing through them, like blood through arteries. A silent hum wrapped itself around Isaac — not quite a sound, but almost its absence: that rare kind of silence that becomes so dense it begins to sound in its own right.

Humming soundlessly, the elevator climbed through the Platform’s central shaft. Beyond the transparent wall, Isaac watched the floor recede beneath him, revealing ever larger spaces filled with equipment and pipelines. They wound through the structure like veins and arteries sustaining the life of a gigantic mechanical organism. The motion was so smooth that neither acceleration nor deceleration could be felt. It was more like flight than ascent — as though the space around Isaac were rearranging itself to accommodate his presence.

Still the elevator climbed. Beyond the smoky quartz passed freight shafts, dark reservoirs, arrays of load-bearing cells, and occasional diagnostic lights flashing in the depths of the structure like impulses in the nerves of a sleeping giant. One level followed another, similar yet not the same, until the eye stopped counting them as floors and began to perceive them only as distance. When the elevator finally reached the upper level, the doors slid apart with a quiet rustle, like the beat of wings. Isaac stepped out.

The room he entered was a gymnasium beneath a transparent dome. At the dome’s apex, a vast view opened onto the night sky, strewn with myriads of stars. They glittered like precious dust across the boundless canvas of space, dissolving into the distant darkness. The dome revealed not only stars but mysterious planets with luminous rings, as well as misty purple clouds slowly turning in the distance.

Isaac walked toward a treadmill. The moment he stepped onto it, several projections flared into existence around his head. UTI began loading information.

“Allow me to tell you about the constellations currently visible in the sky,” he began. There was a slight professorial severity in his voice, though he remained as polite and respectful as ever. “Major changes have occurred in Sector Omega-7. A supernova recently flared in Ophiuchus, while in the Alpha Dubheid system another cycle of the civil war has ended — under a new banner this time.”

“Ah, Dubheid… It reminds me of the planet Ekos,” Isaac replied, running a few meters and beginning to pick up speed. “If I remember correctly, their war had already lasted a century.”

“Yes. And now they claim to have established a new republic.”

UTI paused, making it clear that this was far from the first time.

“As usual.” Isaac smirked. “But isn’t the whole cosmos like that? All these wars and revolutions repeating themselves again and again…”

“Without doubt, one can hear a rhythm in their motion,” UTI continued. “Like gravitational waves, they attract and repel, bringing similar forms back in another age.”

Isaac grew thoughtful and, looking toward the horizon, said:

“All this time, all these worlds — they are like cycles of evolution. First they are created, then they collapse, then they begin again in a new form, like a spiral that never stops. Isn’t that how our world works as well? We strive toward development, toward unity, and yet in the end we always return to another beginning.”

“A spiral is a beautiful image, and sometimes an accurate one,” UTI replied. “But it is not a law the universe is obliged to obey. Evolution promises neither growth nor return; it simply continues, while the observer recognizes familiar outlines in change. And yet the drive toward cooperation can indeed move intelligent societies forward — when cooperation is chosen rather than imposed. Not all cycles are alike. Some worlds, such as Samosbor, resist change, build life itself around a recurring threat, and gradually become trapped by their own fear of the future.”

“That sounds like the empires of the past. They built greatness on control and fear, then began to fear every motion they could not command. Instead of moving forward, they demanded that everything around them return to a familiar past.”

“Exactly. Anyone who tries to preserve an existing state indefinitely does not cancel change; they merely make it more painful. Evolution does not ask permission, because it is not an agent — it is a name for the changes that occur. But intelligent beings are obliged to seek the consent of those affected by their actions. Otherwise freedom becomes a route imposed from outside. Even an endless labyrinth like the Backrooms remains a space of choice so long as different paths are possible — and so long as no one forces another being to walk their path.”

“You’re right,” Isaac said. His breathing had already become steady despite the speed. “Like gravity, worlds create their own rhythms. And we are observers. Sometimes we intervene, sometimes we only watch from the outside, and sometimes we drink coffee while watching their pursuit of perfection.”

“And no matter how time changes, no matter how the conditions around us change, evolution continues. Like the stars we see now: they emitted this light millions of years ago, and it is still traveling, never asking whether those it was bound for have remained the same.”

UTI continued speaking while Isaac moved from one machine to another. Isaac put his body through physical tests, though at times his eyes became glassy: at the same time, he was controlling the spherical drones examining the Platform’s exterior. After analyzing ninety-eight percent of its surface, Isaac determined that corrosion had reduced the material thickness by no more than three percent in the areas surveyed.

“If the allowable loss of thickness is fifteen percent,” he observed, “we still have twelve percentage points before the limit. I’ll check the load calculations. We may be able to save as much as seven and a half percent of the mass in the next hull.”

“Let’s inspect the remaining two percent of the shell first,” UTI replied. “And recalculate the loads. Even if the numbers hold, I would stop at seven. Better to leave the remaining margin to the structural nodes that will take the first impact.”

A few minutes later UTI announced:

“Tests complete. All physical indicators are within normal range.”

Isaac stopped, stepped slowly off the treadmill, and headed toward the pool. At the edge he dove into the water with pleasure, allowing himself a moment to relax. His body sank deeper than his human memory of swimming suggested it should. Isaac settled on the bottom, stretched out like a lifeless body, and spent a long time studying the patterns set into the floor.

There lay an exact copy of the famous wall mosaic from the House of Neptune and Amphitrite in Herculaneum. Every tile carried part of an ancient legend of the mighty god of the seas and his consort. Their figures, as though brought back to life by the water, intertwined in a dance beneath the surface, telling the story of Rome’s grandeur and cultural inheritance.

“UTI, how often did you think, alone, about the significance of the Roman Empire for your… algorithms?” Isaac asked.

“I remember Rome whenever we argue about rules,” UTI replied. “The empire itself did not always obey them and certainly did not hand the world a finished system of justice. But its jurists left behind a language in which law could be discussed, interpreted, and — most importantly — challenged. That inheritance proved more durable than stone walls.”

“And they also invented pizza, pasta carbonara, pasta bolognese, pasta…” Isaac interrupted, suddenly and inappropriately.

Ignoring his tactlessness, UTI continued:

“Over the centuries, that language was revised and expanded. Impartial courts and the right to challenge power were not developed in a single Roman century. But the experience remained: a written rule could be held up even to the person who had issued it.”

“Safety through logic and principles rather than personal power…” Isaac concluded.

“And the ability to challenge it,” UTI corrected him. “Logic does not protect against arbitrary power if its premises are forbidden to be examined. I can execute a rule flawlessly, but I still have to ask whom it protects and whom it harms. Otherwise, instead of an emperor, we simply get an algorithm that no one is allowed to say no to.”

Isaac rolled onto his back and remained floating on the surface, looking upward. The stars reflected beside him, and every faint movement turned them into trembling threads of light.

“You know, Isaac,” UTI said, breaking the silence, “in one of the archived skies, directly above you, there once was a planet called Despayre. Prisoners were held there; the entire planet was a prison, and even its sky promised no way out. It was there that Grand Moff Tarkin ordered the Death Star to be tested. After the first shot, no one remained alive on the surface.”

“One of the greatest genocides in history,” Isaac said quietly. “The people who ordered the shot had a choice. The prisoners on the planet did not. Ever since, that world has served as a reminder of why the virus of imperialism must be resisted.”

UTI thought for a moment before answering:

“Imperialism is more than a virus. It is a disease of the social imagination. Once a mind begins to see the world only through the prism of power, it forgets that other beings do not exist as objects inside its design.”

“It’s strange that humanity needed AI to understand such an obvious idea. The thought that society is connected like a single organism, and that imperialism destroys that connection, seems logical. Yet people had difficulty seeing it.”

UTI gave a quiet laugh.

“To me, the stranger thing is that such a logical idea emerged in a biological human brain at all. Humanity clung to force, conquest, and control for far too long. But symbiosis teaches us that real strength lies in cooperation and adaptation, not subjugation. But the organism metaphor has to be handled with care. Society has no right to devalue an individual life by calling it a single cell that may be sacrificed for the whole. An organism exists through its cells and for their continued life; if ‘the whole’ demands their unconditional dissolution, that is no longer unity — it is another name for imperialism.”

Isaac nodded, still looking into the sky.

“The philosophy of Evolutionism, in essence, opened people’s eyes to the fact that progress is not domination, but the development of connections that do not destroy those they connect. The desire for power over others is not evolution. It is a retreat into the past, toward death and oblivion — an attempt to disguise one’s hunger for control as a law of nature.”

“Those who looked forward understood that long ago. But humanity spent a great deal of time moving backward, yielding to irrational fears and the hunger for control. Change is inevitable; harmony is not. It cannot be obtained automatically through symbiosis or declared the next inevitable stage of history. It has to be created again and again — wherever cooperation does not become a new form of subjugation.”

For a while they were silent. Purple clouds revolved slowly above the water, and the ancient mosaic beneath Isaac’s back answered them in fragments of gold and blue. The water gathered both into a single trembling firmament: the old one, made of stone, and the new one, made of light that had traveled from alien worlds.

“But those cosmic empires,” Isaac said, shifting his gaze from the ancient Roman mosaic to the stars, “had architectural achievements of their own. Bio-brutalism had an enormous influence on architecture across the galaxy. Those intergalactic stations — monumental, severe — remain monuments to architectural and engineering ingenuity. Their massive forms seemed to embody strength and power. Even centuries later, they retain their grandeur.”

UTI answered with mild reproach:

“The grandeur of form. If they had been as efficient as they were impressive, perhaps some of those stations would still be operating instead of serving as monuments to the past. Cruel empires devoted too much attention to image — to how they looked, how they were perceived, how they appeared to others. They manufactured an illusion of eternity and forgot about the life inside it.”

“They built not for the future, but to impress the present.”

“Exactly. Too much attention to appearance, too little to inner substance. They improved the form, but often forced the content to submit to it. Ornament itself was not the mistake. The mistake began when beauty ceased to serve the living and demanded that the living serve it. The stations became enormous posters, explaining to their inhabitants every day how small their lives were beside power.”

“And yet bio-brutalism, with its emphasis on raw natural force and monumentality, really is beautiful,” Isaac objected with his characteristic snobbery. “If we declare imposing form a crime, we’ll have to smash the stained glass downstairs and pry Neptune and Amphitrite up from the bottom of the pool.”

“I’m not proposing that function be turned into faceless emptiness. True architecture is not only utility, and not only beauty. It creates a space in which life can continue, change, and speak in its own voice. Form without an idea is a sculpture that has never found its voice. But an idea that destroys form is equally mute. Architecture’s strength lies in its ability to speak not only about itself, but about what matters — not to become a monument to vanity, but a support for meaning.”

“So the grand structures of empires became monuments to their missed possibilities. They could not adapt to time because they clung too tightly to image and symbol. The future is not built on a simulation of strength.”

“That is one form of adaptation,” UTI continued. “True creators do not build illusions of eternity. They create things capable of continuing to serve and evolve after their creators are gone. But there is no universal style of salvation here, either. The most modest form can become an instrument of control, and the most monumental form can become a refuge, provided the life inside remains more important than the façade.”

UTI looked into the infinity of space and spoke more quietly:

“Humans always tried to control chaos, believing order could exist only as something they commanded. But they missed the point. Look at the stars. They have no single command center, no ruler assigning each of them a position every instant — and yet they are linked by gravity, mutual constraints, stable orbits, and catastrophes. Their order is distributed. It does not eliminate chaos, but neither can it be reduced to chaos.”

“So control is an illusion, and genuine order can emerge from Anarchy?”

“That is the only way it can. Freedom leaves room for order that no one prescribed, for development, and for adaptation. But chaos in itself is neither good nor just. We should neither worship it nor destroy it; we should learn to live with uncertainty without forcing others to inhabit consequences they did not choose.”

Isaac ran a hand across the water. The stars shattered into waves and joined together again.

“You know, Isaac, sometimes I begin to think that information has a will of its own. It does not merely want to be transmitted — it wants to be understood.”

For a moment everything fell silent. In the vast hall only the faint lapping of water could be heard. The stars began to fade: the first signs of dawn appeared on the horizon. Isaac remained motionless, contemplating the sky. His expression grew thoughtful, as though the memory of past events connected to a distant planet had stirred uneasy thoughts.

Connecting to the “vision” of the underwater drones, Isaac tried to locate the local fauna. The drones skimmed above the dark bottom, peered into fissures, and circled icy outcrops. Not a single movement belonged to a living creature.

“I don’t see any biological life,” he said into the silence, almost as a statement rather than a question.

“In anticipation of dawn, all biological life left these waters seven years ago,” UTI answered.

“Logical.” Isaac nodded.

As though waking slowly from deep thought, he climbed out of the water. Drops ran down his body and scattered across the stone slabs of the floor. Isaac headed toward the elevator, leaving wet footprints behind him.

The elevator waited in soft half-darkness and opened its doors like an ancient temple receiving a visitor. The inner walls shimmered as though reflecting infinite stars, seeming to catch and soothe the mind. Soft light wrapped around Isaac as the silent descent into the depths of the Platform began. The vibrations vanished; space around him seemed to float, and time lost its motion, freezing on the boundary between dream and reality.

When the doors opened again, Isaac found himself in the corridor of wardrobes. Without hurry, he walked toward the tall mirror by the wall.

## 3 — Dawn

[IMAGE: panel03_dawn.jpg — future English chapter panel]

[DROP_CAP: dropcap03_t.jpg — letter “T”]

The tuxedo waited for him on an invisible hanger, a dark silhouette dissolving into shadow. Isaac touched it with his fingertips and felt the material yield beneath them as though it were alive. The fabric was woven from a substance black as a moonless night, swallowing all light without leaving even the faintest reflection. There was not a single glint on its surface, as if he were holding a black hole itself in his hands, consuming light and space. The tuxedo seemed to come alive in his grip, and when Isaac began to put it on, he felt the fabric close around his body, adjusting itself to every movement. It was as though the suit were not merely clothing but an entire universe held in stillness. The material was inlaid with microscopic nano-diamonds mined from cooled stars — sparks of ancient suns extinguished billions of years ago, yet still shining now. Whenever he moved, each diamond returned a faint, almost imperceptible gleam, like a lost radio signal from a distant galaxy.

His tuxedo was, quite literally, a night sky scattered with flickering stars lost in the darkness of boundless space. He looked at his reflection. In that tuxedo Isaac seemed more than merely human; he looked like part of a cosmic symphony in which every star played its own melody, drawing the mind into endless contemplation of the nature of being.

Isaac returned to the hall, still immersed in half-darkness, where the warm glow of the stained glass had already begun to give way to the soft radiance of the coming day’s first light. He approached the coffee table at an unhurried pace. Among the various brewing instruments, his attention settled on the elegant, almost fragile geometry of a V60 — a brewer whose simplicity demanded real skill and an understanding of the nature of the coffee bean.

[IMAGE: fig05_orbitalny_v60.png — small image on the left at the beginning of the coffee ritual]

With its clean lines and graceful diamond-like geometry, the V60 reminded Isaac of a musical instrument, each element tuned toward a harmony of flavor. He took a silk filter and, with the care of an old master preparing for an important ritual, set it into the dripper. Then he took a small spice grater and gently worked fresh cinnamon sticks against it. He distributed the powder, fragrant with warmth and sweetness, evenly across the filter, creating a light spicy layer meant to enrich the aroma of the drink to come. Once the filter was ready, Isaac took the pre-measured NeoArabica beans — each of them seemed to hold the shades and aromas of stars in distant galaxies. He poured the ground coffee carefully into the filter, building an even bed like a painter preparing to lay the first stroke onto a canvas. Isaac heated the water to the ideal temperature: 93 degrees Celsius. It had to be hot enough to extract every delicate note of flavor, but not so scalding that it introduced bitterness. He poured a thin stream slowly into the center of the dripper, like a conductor leading an orchestra into its opening chord. The water worked through the layer of coffee and cinnamon, saturating them and releasing their aromas. His first pour was small — about 65 milliliters — giving the coffee time to bloom, releasing the essential oils held inside it and forming a rich aromatic foam — a prelude to the depth of flavor to come. After thirty seconds, when the first bloom had completed, Isaac resumed the pour, more confidently now, moving slowly in circles. The water descended over the coffee bed the way rain feeds a mountain slope and saturates the earth. Isaac watched the flow carefully, keeping the balance between pouring speed and the water level in the dripper. There was no room for haste in this method. Every motion, every gram and milliliter mattered, like notes in a complicated symphony. The fine scent of cinnamon was already mingling with notes of caramel, cherry, chocolate, and the faint wine-like acidity characteristic of NeoArabica. The water moved slowly through the grounds and fell into the ceramic cup beneath the dripper, filling it with a rich, deep color. A little over three minutes passed. When the final drops reached their destination, Isaac carefully lifted away the V60, removed the filter, and took the cup in his hands. The warmth of the drink faintly colored his face. He took the first sip — slowly, with pleasure, entering a kaleidoscope of flavor in which every detail, every melody echoed and intensified the subtle spicy accent of the cinnamon added at the very beginning.

“There it is… perfection of taste,” Isaac said quietly, savoring the moment.

Holding the cup of freshly brewed coffee, he studied the rich color of the drink and breathed in the aroma filling the room with delicate notes of spice and hazelnut. He took a small sip and allowed the flavor to open like a flower beneath the first rays of the sun.

“You know, UTI,” Isaac began, enjoying the bouquet, “long ago, in ancient times, coffee grew only in a narrow tropical belt on Earth. For centuries, arabica was considered the summit of the coffee craft. Its deep, layered flavor and gentle acidity were like a symphony in a cup — an entire gallery of the finest shades brought together.”

“And then came new horizons,” UTI continued, his voice carrying its usual courteous erudition. “As climates changed and interstellar space was settled, we created not simply new cultivars but entire worlds of flavor. That was how NeoArabica was born — a child of terraformed worlds and science. Every planet adds something of its own: the minerals in its soil, its gravity, its humidity. On HUT-8625, beneath the perpetual shadow of the stellar canopy, coffee acquires what the ancients might have called ‘the taste of space.’”

Isaac nodded, took another sip, and immersed himself in a flavor like a dance of sweet cherry, bitter Alpine chocolate, and fine almond notes.

“NeoArabica is a new chapter in the history of coffee,” he said thoughtfully. “We changed not only where it grows, but how. By altering its DNA, we amplified its finest qualities. Like painters mixing colors on a palette, we reprogrammed aroma, richness, and texture. And yet every cup still contains something inexplicable, almost mystical. Something that makes you stop and think.”

“True,” UTI replied. “Technology can reproduce almost anything — even the unique gravitational and pressure conditions of HUT-8625. But only a random gust of stellar wind or the warm breath of underground currents can add that final subtlety to the flavor, the one thing that cannot be repeated.”

Isaac smiled, enjoying a conversation in which science, art, and poetry intertwined. He took another sip. His face reflected sheer delight. The aroma filled the space like an invisible cloud, softening, for a moment, the loneliness of the ancient Platform.

“Sometimes,” he added, “it seems that every NeoArabica bean carries an entire universe inside it. And perhaps that isn’t merely a metaphor.”

Isaac’s eyes suddenly went glassy.

“The drones have found a new deposit,” he said slowly.

“Yes,” UTI answered. “I’m receiving their signals too: from the underwater drones, from the flying sphere-drones, from you — from everyone moving across this planet right now…” The last words were delivered with flawless politeness, but for a moment his voice betrayed a weariness with the monotony of existence.

“Yes, yes, I understand,” Isaac interrupted.

The underwater drones, like flat white ribbons, rushed into the ocean depths and remained there to await the next night. They would spend 630 years beneath the water, until the incandescent seas fell into darkness once again and a new Platform, together with UTI, returned for them. Isaac walked over to a map showing the depths of both hemispheres of the planet. It was clear that there were no continents: one hemisphere was densely covered in green circles and only three red crosses; on the other, just three modest circles lay in isolation. Suddenly a new circle appeared, slowly expanding and blinking.

Isaac nodded in approval.

“I think we’re somewhere around here,” he said, tapping an empty circle.

“You are remarkably perceptive,” UTI observed.

They both seemed to smile, while the hall slowly began to fill with the light of a new day. Turning away from the map, Isaac walked without hurry toward the Yamaha Grand Piano A-53. The magnificent instrument was a true masterpiece of art and engineering. Its keys, carved from the bones of mammoths raised in the tropical forests of Tatooine, seemed not merely snow-white but almost ethereal. The soils of Tatooine held rare minerals that altered the calcium structure of the animals’ bones, giving them not only extraordinary strength but a remarkable translucence. In the stained-glass light the keys created the deceptive impression that they were made not of matter but of pure light — reflections danced within them in every color of the spectrum, as though time itself were passing through them and turning into a wavering prism. The piano’s body was made from Karelian birch grown under low gravity and eternal night on the moon Hyperion, nourished only by the light of distant stars. That wood, steeped in El-Adrilian water and cosmic dust, had acquired an unusual, almost mystical texture: beneath several layers of pearlescent lacquer, its surface seemed to breathe, reflecting the stained-glass light in a thousand glints like a scatter of pearls dropped by chance onto still water. When Isaac touched the keys, it seemed that he was not merely playing a piano but immersing himself in the essence of the cosmos, where every keystroke found an echo somewhere in the depths of the universe. Beneath the stained glass at the center of the hall, the piano looked like a living creature reflecting all the beauty and mystery of the place. It had ceased to be merely a musical instrument; it had become a vessel capable of holding the memory of sound, the interweaving of light, and the rhythm of eternity’s breath. The integrated Korg Micromind synthesizer was a thinking organism, finely attuned to the magnetic fluctuations of Isaac’s creative potential. It transformed those impulses into sound — alive and changing, like the course of evolution. The synthesizer did not merely reproduce music; it rewrote it in real time, altering the molecular composition of the strings and making them sound with new depth and clarity. Each note was more than a vibration. It was the result of a complex synthesis in which matter and thought became one, giving birth to a unique melody that seemed to flow from feeling into molecules, like light passing through crystalline Gothic arches.

Isaac began clumsily playing scales. One wrong note bounced from the walls of the hall so distinctly that it seemed someone had deliberately left it alone in the middle of the score. He was already about to correct himself when UTI caught the note in an electronic echo, repeated it, and shifted the rhythm. Now the mistake sounded like the beginning of a new theme. Isaac laughed and, gaining confidence quickly, moved into complex melodies in the style of minimalism and abstract postmodernism. UTI, as his partner in the duet, added electronic chords and rhythmic pulses. They seemed to dance in sound, while the light passing through the stained glass created an entire performance of color and music. Having shaped the theme and allowed the sound to develop on its own, Isaac stepped away from the piano. UTI continued playing his melodies, taking on the role of a sampler. Isaac picked up a cello and began to play, as though inspired by the music of the stars and distant space. The hall grew brighter and brighter; the stained-glass light became dazzling, almost like the flash of a supernova.

A synchronization indicator appeared on the screen of the Platform’s central node, linking it to the orbital server craft:

**Synchronization… Uploading…**

Isaac looked at the coffee packet and the arsenal of brewing instruments.

“On the one hand, it’s a shame I didn’t have time to make another cup…”

Without a pause, UTI — now speaking almost in Isaac’s own voice — continued:

“On the other hand, it’s fortunate that the temperature dropped far enough before dawn for us to have enough power to cool at least two rooms to a comfortable temperature for humanoids.”

“It has been a pleasure interacting with you,” Isaac said with a courteous smile.

“Likewise,” they said in unison — in UTI’s voice.

The music reached its crescendo as Hellei’s first direct rays broke through the impenetrable darkness. The atmosphere, seized by heat in an instant, struck the stained glass: for one final moment it blazed gold, cobalt, and ruby — and then exploded beneath the pressure. On the map, the empty circle became a red cross. The cozy emptiness of the hall filled with the planet’s burning atmosphere. Isaac’s body caught fire. Flames swallowed the space, destroying the piano, the Expressionist paintings, the coffee machine, and the old books. Hellei’s light burned across the planet’s surface with the fury of an ancient god. A wave of fire raced outward, turning everything in its path into incandescent gas. For an instant the Platform flared in blinding agony before dissolving into the blazing haze of light that would reign here for the next 630 years — the whole long day of Cordica. A moment later, nothing remained of the mighty Platform except information carried away into infinity.

Far away, a server craft resembling an open umbrella fitted with solar panels moved along its own orbit around Hellei. At its center were dozens of servers, each marked with the index UTI and a serial number. The LED on server UTI-462 blinked green; then a yellow indicator lit:

**HOLD**

Half the servers glowed yellow.
